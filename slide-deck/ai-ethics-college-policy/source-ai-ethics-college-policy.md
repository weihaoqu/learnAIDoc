# AI Ethics in College Assignments

Primary wiki title:
AI Ethics in College: Stop Asking "Did Students Use AI?" Ask Whether They Learned

Primary thesis:
The college AI debate should move beyond forbid/allow. A fair policy should allow AI as a learning partner, require meaningful disclosure, protect private and unauthorized material, and assess understanding rather than polished output alone.

Opening case:
A professor assigns a short report. The syllabus says "use AI responsibly and disclose assistance" but does not define responsibility, approved tools, privacy boundaries, or what evidence proves learning. One student uses a paid model and cannot defend the final work. One student avoids AI and faces a time/access tradeoff. One student uploads instructor materials and peer comments into an unapproved tool. The professor sees polished work, vague disclosure, and uncertain understanding.

Four paper basis:
- Jobin, Ienca, and Vayena 2019: AI ethics guideline review; recurring principles include transparency, fairness, responsibility, privacy, and non-maleficence, but shared words do not imply shared implementation.
- Corrêa et al. 2023: 200 AI governance documents; many principles appear, but governance is often soft and implementation/enforcement remain limited.
- Giarmoleo et al. 2024: systematic literature review; AI ethics solutions split between act-centered rules and agent-centered formation/judgment.
- Elizabeth M. Groen, Tamar Sharon, and Marcel Becker 2026: AI ethics through principles, lived realities, and power structures.

Policy model:
- Allow: AI for brainstorming, explanation, planning, organization, feedback, study support, and revision when the assignment permits it.
- Disclose: meaningful assistance, not just "I used ChatGPT."
- Protect: no unauthorized instructor materials, peer work, private data, grades, feedback, unpublished research, or confidential materials in unapproved tools.
- Assess: grade report, disclosure, prompts/categories, verification, presentation, Q&A, quiz/defense, and demo when relevant.

Boundary:
Allowed: AI helps discuss and organize ideas; student writes, verifies, and defends.
Borderline: AI generates ideas or first drafts that the student studies, revises, verifies, and owns.
Prohibited: AI completes the assignment and the student cannot explain, verify, or defend it.

Homework:
Students write a 900-1200 word report using AI only as a thinking partner. They submit final report, AI disclosure appendix, three prompt excerpts or prompt summaries, source verification checklist, and one-page reflection. In class they present for 5 minutes and answer peer/instructor questions.
